Supercritical Units

660 MW Supercritical Unit Operations — Key Parameters & Interview Questions

By a working Assistant Engineer, 500 MW Thermal Power Station · Published August 2026 · 9 min read

Over the last decade, India's power sector has been shifting its new capacity additions from the older 500 MW subcritical fleet towards 660 MW and 700 MW supercritical units. If you've trained on a subcritical unit — like the 500 MW drum-type boiler I operate day to day — a supercritical unit looks familiar on the outside but runs on a genuinely different thermodynamic principle inside. NTPC and most state gencos now expect AE/JE candidates to know this distinction, so here's a working engineer's explanation of what actually changes.

What Does "Supercritical" Actually Mean?

Water has a critical point — around 221.2 bar and 374°C — above which there is no distinct boundary between liquid water and steam; the fluid moves smoothly from a dense liquid-like state to a light vapour-like state without ever "boiling" in the traditional sense. A subcritical unit, like a standard 500 MW boiler, always operates below this pressure, so it needs a steam drum to physically separate saturated water from saturated steam. A supercritical unit operates above the critical pressure, so that separation is no longer physically possible — and that single fact reshapes the entire boiler design.

Once-Through Boiler Design — No Steam Drum

This is the most exam-relevant difference: a supercritical boiler is a once-through (Benson-type) boiler. Feedwater enters the economiser, passes once through the furnace waterwall tubes, and emerges directly as superheated steam — there is no drum anywhere in the circuit to recirculate or separate water from steam.

Feature500 MW Subcritical (Drum-Type)660 MW Supercritical (Once-Through)
Boiler circuitNatural/assisted circulation through a steam drumOnce-through; no drum
Water-steam separationPhysical separation in the drumNo separation needed above the critical point
Startup circulationNatural circulation once boiling startsNeeds a Boiler Circulating Pump (BCP) at low loads/startup
Feedwater purity requirementStringentEven more stringent — no drum to "buffer" impurities
Furnace waterwall tubesVertical, larger diameterOften spiral-wound, smaller diameter, higher mass flux

Because there's no drum to fall back on during low-load operation, most supercritical units run a Boiler Circulating Pump (BCP) below roughly 30-35% load to force enough flow through the waterwalls and keep tube metal temperatures safe. Above that load, flow is high enough that the BCP is normally stopped and the unit runs in pure once-through mode.

Typical Operating Parameters

Exact figures vary by design (BHEL, Toshiba/JSW, Doosan and others all supply this class of unit) and by coal quality, so treat the numbers below as representative ranges for comparison rather than a specific plant's exact figures.

Parameter500 MW Subcritical (typical)660 MW Supercritical (typical)
Main steam pressure~170 kg/cm²(a)~245-250 kg/cm²(a)
Main steam temperature~537°C~537-565°C
Reheat steam temperature~537°C~565°C
Approx. cycle/turbine efficiency~38-40%~42-45%
Coal consumption at full loadLower (scaled to capacity)Higher in absolute tonnes/day, but lower per MWh generated
ESP efficiency>99.9%>99.9%

The higher pressure and temperature aren't arbitrary — they directly raise the average temperature at which heat is added in the Rankine cycle, which is what pushes cycle efficiency up. A few percentage points of efficiency translate into real coal savings and lower CO2 emissions per unit generated, which is exactly why India's newer capacity additions are almost all supercritical or ultra-supercritical.

Feedwater System Protection Philosophy

Whether a unit is subcritical or supercritical, the feedwater train — deaerator and condenser hotwell — still needs the same layered level-protection philosophy, because both equipment can flood or run dry regardless of boiler type:

  • Lo-Lo level (trip): Protects pumps from running dry / cavitating — typically interlocked to trip or block dependent equipment such as boiler feed pumps.
  • Lo alarm: Warns the operator before the trip point is reached, giving time to correct makeup/extraction balance.
  • Normal level band: The operating target maintained by level control valves during steady-state running.
  • Hi alarm: Warns of an upward trend, often linked to an overflow/reject valve opening automatically.
  • Hi-Hi level (trip): Protects against water carryover into steam lines or turbine — typically the highest-priority interlock in the loop.

The exact millimetre setpoints differ from plant to plant and are set out in each unit's own C&I documentation — what matters for exam purposes is understanding why this five-tier structure (Lo-Lo / Lo / Normal / Hi / Hi-Hi) exists on every major level loop in the plant, supercritical or not.

Interview Questions & Answers

Q1. Why doesn't a supercritical boiler have a steam drum?

A: Above the critical pressure of water (~221.2 bar), there is no distinct liquid and vapour phase to separate, so a drum — whose only job is to separate saturated water from saturated steam — serves no physical purpose. The boiler instead uses a once-through (Benson-type) design.

Q2. What is a Boiler Circulating Pump (BCP) and when is it used?

A: A BCP forces additional water flow through the furnace waterwall tubes during startup and low-load operation (typically below ~30-35% load), when natural flow from the once-through design alone isn't enough to keep tube metal temperatures safe. It's normally taken out of service once load — and therefore natural flow — is high enough.

Q3. Why is feedwater purity even more critical in a supercritical unit than a subcritical one?

A: A drum in a subcritical unit gives impurities somewhere to concentrate and be blown down; a once-through boiler has no such buffer, so any dissolved solids or silica travel straight through to the superheater and turbine, risking deposition and blade fouling.

Q4. Why does higher steam pressure and temperature improve cycle efficiency?

A: In a Rankine cycle, efficiency improves as the average temperature at which heat is added to the working fluid rises. Higher boiler pressure and temperature raise this average, so supercritical units extract more work per unit of heat input than subcritical units.

Q5. What is the practical difference between "supercritical" and "ultra-supercritical"?

A: Both are once-through, drum-less designs. "Ultra-supercritical" simply refers to units operating at even higher steam temperatures (commonly 593°C and above), pushing efficiency further — the terms describe a spectrum rather than a hard category boundary.

Quick Revision Points

  • Supercritical = operating above water's critical point (~221.2 bar, 374°C) — no liquid/vapour phase distinction.
  • Once-through (Benson-type) boiler, no steam drum, unlike subcritical drum-type units.
  • Boiler Circulating Pump (BCP) assists flow at startup/low load; stopped at higher loads.
  • Higher pressure/temperature → higher average heat-addition temperature → higher Rankine cycle efficiency.
  • Feedwater purity requirements are stricter with no drum to buffer impurities.
  • Deaerator/hotwell level protection follows the same Lo-Lo/Lo/Normal/Hi/Hi-Hi philosophy regardless of boiler type.
